What Is the Hardest Month of Sobriety?

By the Steady team · Published November 27, 2026 · Updated November 27, 2026

Short answer: For many people the first month is the hardest, because withdrawal and the strongest cravings come early. Alcohol withdrawal usually starts within 8 hours of the last drink and peaks around 24 to 72 hours. Many people hit a second rough patch around months 2 to 4, when sleep problems, low mood and anxiety can linger. Everyone's timeline is different.

Safety first: stopping alcohol suddenly after heavy, regular drinking can cause dangerous withdrawal, including seizures. Talk to a doctor before you stop, or call the SAMHSA National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357. If someone has a seizure, severe confusion or hallucinations, call 911 or your local emergency number.

Month 1: the hardest stretch for most people

The first month carries the most physical and practical change. According to MedlinePlus, alcohol withdrawal usually begins within 8 hours of the last drink, peaks around 24 to 72 hours, and can go on for weeks. Common symptoms include anxiety, shakiness, sweating, poor sleep, irritability and a fast heartbeat. The Cleveland Clinic gives the same 24 to 72 hour peak.

Even after physical symptoms ease, the rest of the month is often about routines. Evenings, weekends, payday and social plans can all feel built around drinking. Cravings tend to be strong and frequent, and sleep may take a while to settle.

Months 2 to 4: the second dip

Many people expect each week to get easier and are surprised by a slump in the second or third month. Some describe this as post-acute withdrawal syndrome (PAWS), or protracted withdrawal: symptoms such as low mood, anxiety, irritability, poor sleep and trouble concentrating that last after acute withdrawal ends.

The evidence on timing varies. A 2022 systematic review of protracted alcohol withdrawal found mood and anxiety symptoms reported over the first 3 to 4 months, and sleep problems lasting up to about 6 months. SAMHSA's advisory on protracted withdrawal notes that it isn't a formal diagnosis in the DSM. The Hazelden Betty Ford Foundation says symptoms often peak in the first few months and fade gradually. At the same time, the early motivation and novelty can wear off, which is part of why this stretch feels hard.

How to get through the hard months

  • Plan for your trigger times. Most cravings cluster around the same hours, days and situations. Write down when they hit so you can plan around them.
  • Keep your reasons close. A short note in your own words, read in a hard moment, can carry you past a craving.
  • Ride out cravings. A craving usually rises, peaks and passes. Slow breathing, a walk or a call can fill the gap.
  • Protect your sleep. Keep regular bed and wake times; poor sleep makes everything else harder.
  • Tell someone. A friend, a sponsor, a group or a counselor. You don't have to white-knuckle it alone.
  • Count what you've gained. Days, money saved and milestones are concrete proof of progress on a flat day.

Common questions

Does sobriety get easier after 30 days?

For many people the physical side gets easier after the first few weeks, but cravings and mood can still dip in months 2 to 4. Knowing that dip is common makes it easier to plan for.

What is PAWS?

Post-acute withdrawal syndrome describes symptoms such as low mood, anxiety, poor sleep and trouble concentrating that continue after acute withdrawal. It isn't a formal DSM diagnosis, and how long it lasts varies from person to person.

When do alcohol cravings stop?

There's no fixed date. Cravings usually become less frequent and less intense over the first months, but can return around stress, holidays or old routines. Tracking when they happen helps you prepare.

Is the first week of sobriety dangerous?

It can be after heavy, regular drinking, because severe alcohol withdrawal can cause seizures and delirium. Talk to a doctor before stopping, and call emergency services for seizures or severe confusion.
